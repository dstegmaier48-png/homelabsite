# Biomeiler

A *Biomeiler*—often described in English as a compost heater—is a large, moist pile of woody and other organic material. During aerobic composting, microorganisms break down the biomass and release heat. A water-filled pipe or another heat exchanger inside the pile can collect part of this low-temperature energy and transport it to where it is needed.

Research on compost heat-recovery systems shows that biologically active piles can reach thermophilic temperatures around 50–65 °C. The useful output depends strongly on pile size, feedstock, moisture, oxygen supply, insulation and heat-exchanger design.

![Biomeiler made from organic material inside a wire enclosure, with a weather cover]({{ '/assets/images/hidde-gardening/biomeiler/biomeiler-construction.jpg' | relative_url }})

## The basic idea

1. Woody cuttings, straw, leaves or similar biomass are mixed and moistened.
2. The material is assembled into a sufficiently large, aerated pile.
3. Aerobic microorganisms convert part of the biomass and generate heat.
4. Water circulates through pipework embedded in or connected to the pile.
5. The warmed water transfers energy to a buffer tank or a low-temperature heating application.
6. After the active phase, the decomposed material can be matured and used as a soil amendment where suitable.

![Straw-covered Biomeiler with visible water pipework]({{ '/assets/images/hidde-gardening/biomeiler/biomeiler-pipework.jpg' | relative_url }})

## Potential uses for the heat

- Preheating domestic hot water
- Frost protection or heating for a greenhouse
- Low-temperature space or underfloor heating
- Warming an animal shelter or workshop
- Maintaining the operating temperature of a separate anaerobic digester

The available temperature and heating power vary over time. A Biomeiler is therefore better suited to steady, low-temperature demand or preheating than to applications requiring guaranteed high-temperature output.

## Biogas: a separate anaerobic process

The aerated compost pile produces heat, but it is not itself a controlled biogas reactor. Usable biogas requires organic material to be broken down without oxygen inside a sealed anaerobic digester. The warm Biomeiler can surround or heat that digester and help its microorganisms remain active during cold weather.

Anaerobic digestion produces:

- **Biogas**, mainly methane and carbon dioxide, which can be treated and used for heat, electricity or fuel
- **Digestate**, a nutrient-containing solid and liquid residue that may be processed for use as fertiliser or soil amendment

This combination follows the general Jean Pain concept: aerobic composting provides heat, while a separate oxygen-free vessel provides methane-rich gas. The two biological processes complement one another but must remain technically distinct.

![Close-up showing moisture and gas bubbles in the experimental setup]({{ '/assets/images/hidde-gardening/biomeiler/digestion-close-up.jpg' | relative_url }})

## Benefits

- Converts locally available organic residues into useful heat
- Can reduce demand for conventional heating during the active composting period
- Can support biogas production by warming a separate digester
- Produces composted material or digestate that may return nutrients and organic matter to the soil
- Uses relatively simple components for small experimental installations

## Safety

Biogas contains flammable methane and may contain toxic hydrogen sulphide. A gas-producing system needs purpose-built gas-tight equipment, pressure relief, controlled ventilation, leak detection and appropriate gas treatment. Open flames and ignition sources must be kept away, and applicable construction, environmental and fire-safety rules must be checked before operation. The photographs document an experiment and are not construction instructions.
